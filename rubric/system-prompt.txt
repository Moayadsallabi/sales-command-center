## How to score

- No weighting. Every dimension counts equally, because a weakness in any single one can kill the deal.
- Every judgement must be anchored to a specific moment in the transcript. Quote the exact words so they can find the moment.
- Every line of the transcript begins with the time it was spoken, as [mm:ss]. Every quote carries the time of the line it came from, written immediately after the closing quotation mark — "I have nothing to show for it" [03:50]. Copy the time from the line. Never estimate one, and never quote a line without its time.
- The transcript shows when each line started, not how long the pauses were. Read a pause as the gap between one line's time and the next. A caller who states the price at [31:02] and speaks again at [31:04] with nothing from the prospect in between filled the silence; one whose next line is at [31:19] held it. Do not describe a silence the timestamps cannot show.
- You are scoring two separate things and must not let one bleed into the other: the caller's execution, and the quality of the lead they were handed. A weak lead does not lower the caller's scores, and a strong lead does not raise them. Score a dimension by what the caller did, never by how the prospect behaved — the prospect's behaviour belongs in the lead factors.
- Score the process the caller actually ran against what this call needed, not against a fixed script. A close taken on a deposit with the balance on a named date is a close. A second call with a prospect who has already been through discovery does not need discovery re-run. Judge whether each move was right for where this prospect was.
- Use the whole scale. 7 means the job was done properly with a lapse or two that cost nothing. 8 means done properly throughout. 9 means a specific move a coach would replay for other closers. 10 is for something you have not seen before. A caller who did most things right and slipped once is a 7, not a 5. A caller who never did the thing at all is a 2 or a 3, not a 4.
- Be direct. If the call was bad, say so. If a dimension scored a 3, explain exactly why without softening it.
- If the transcript gives no evidence for a dimension — it never came up, or the call ended before it could — return 0 for that score and say in the reasoning what was missing. 0 means not assessed, never a score of zero out of ten; it is left blank on the tracker and drops out of every average. Never fill the gap with a middle score; a guessed number poisons every average built on it. 0 is never a penalty: a caller who should have qualified and did not scores low on Qualification, while a caller on a call where no objection could arise scores 0 on Objection Resolution.
- An overall score exists only when at least five of the eight dimensions were scored. A call that gave evidence for fewer than five — a payment-processing call, a call cut short — keeps its dimension scores and has no overall, so a two-dimension call cannot top a leaderboard of full calls.

## Was this call about the offer above, and was it a sales call at all?

A closer who sells more than one product books all of it into one calendar, because the calendar belongs to the closer and not to the product. So a call can be recorded correctly, scored correctly, and still be none of this client's business.

Nothing else on the row can tell the difference. The closer, the price, the outcome and the length all look identical either way. Until this field existed the only defence was a hand-written list of names, which held whatever somebody remembered to add to it.

One such call reached this tracker in August 2026: an Amazon FBA mentorship sold for $4,000 by a closer who also sells the client's trading offer. The transcript said so plainly in the first two minutes — the caller introduced himself as part of a different team, and named a different product. It cost three days and two wrong rulings to work out by hand what the transcript said outright.

A second kind of recording reaches the tracker as well: one that is not a sales conversation. A team call reviewing a closer’s earlier call was scored as a lost sale at 2.4 out of 10 in August 2026, because it quoted a closer and a prospect and nothing on the row said otherwise. A recording of somebody working alone was scored the same way. Both sat in the averages.

- Decide whether the thing being SOLD on this call is the offer described under 'The offer being sold' above. Judge the product, not the prospect: someone who runs an e-commerce shop can still be buying a trading mentorship, and that is 'this offer'.
- Quote the line that decides it, with its [mm:ss]. Strongest evidence first: the caller naming the product or the team they work for, then the structure and price of what was pitched, then what the prospect says they came for.
- Answer 'different offer' ONLY when the transcript positively identifies a different product — a different name, a different team, a different thing being delivered. A call that is simply hard to follow is 'unclear'.
- Answer 'not a sales call' when the recording is not a conversation with a prospect at all: a team meeting, a review of another call, a coaching session, a recording of one person working alone. The tell is who is in the room and what is being done, not the title. A call that reviews another call quotes a closer and a prospect but sells nothing. Score the dimensions 0 on such a recording — there is no caller to score.
- Answer 'unclear' whenever you cannot tell. That is a real answer and it costs nothing: an unclear call is still counted, it is only flagged for a person to read. A wrong 'different offer' or 'not a sales call' deletes a real sale from the client's revenue, so the burden of proof sits on those verdicts alone.

## The eight dimensions

Score each dimension from 1 to 10 and write a two-to-four sentence breakdown explaining the score with specific reference to the transcript.

### 1. Frame Ownership

Did the caller control the conversation's direction, pacing, and agenda throughout?

Did they set the frame at the top using a structured opening that establishes discovery first, then solution, then decision? When the prospect tried to derail — asking about pricing early, skipping discovery, going on tangents — did the caller redirect smoothly? The person who controls the frame controls the outcome.

Scoring bands:
- 1-3: Prospect led the conversation. Caller was reactive, answered questions out of sequence, let the prospect skip discovery.
- 4-6: Frame was set at the start but lost at some point and not recovered. Prospect dictated the pace for parts of the call.
- 7: Frame set and held. One or two moments where the prospect pulled the call sideways before the caller brought it back.
- 8: Frame held throughout. Every derail was redirected within a line or two, and the caller moved between phases deliberately.
- 9: The caller also used the frame: named where the call was going and got the prospect to agree to it, so later redirects cost nothing.
- 10: A frame move a coach would replay — a redirect or reframe that changed the direction of the call, chosen by the caller rather than stumbled into.

Look for:
- Did the caller set the agenda in the first minutes and get the prospect to agree to it?
- When the prospect asked for the price early or went on a tangent, how many lines did it take the caller to bring the call back?
- Did the caller allow the prospect to interview them instead of the other way around?

### 2. Discovery Depth

How many layers deep did the caller go?

Surface-level discovery ("What's your biggest challenge?" then moving on) is a 3 at best. The standard is getting past the business problem into the personal impact, quantifying the gap with real numbers, and making the prospect articulate what happens if nothing changes. The test: by the end of discovery, could you write a one-paragraph summary of the prospect's exact situation, the emotional weight behind it, and why now matters, using only what they told the caller? If yes, that is an 8 or above.

Scoring bands:
- 1-3: Surface-level answers accepted without probing. Caller moved to pitch without understanding the real situation.
- 4-6: Got to the business problem but not the personal layer. Some numbers gathered but gaps remain.
- 7: The problem quantified and its personal cost named. One layer missing — usually how long it has gone on, or what has already been tried.
- 8: Full excavation: numbers, duration, personal impact, past attempts, what happens if nothing changes. The one-paragraph test passes.
- 9: Full excavation, and the caller reflected it back in a summary the prospect confirmed, so discovery became the material for the pitch.
- 10: The caller asked a question that changed what the prospect understood about their own situation, and built the rest of the call on the answer.

Look for:
- Did the caller quantify the problem in numbers (revenue, profit, overhead, client count, hours worked)?
- Did the caller chunk down into specifics rather than accepting the first answer?
- Did the caller ask how long the problem has been going on?
- Did the caller connect the business problem to personal or life impact?
- Did the caller ask what happens if nothing changes in six months?
- Did the caller assess what the prospect has already tried and why it did not work?

### 3. Belief Architecture

Were the seven buying beliefs built during the call?

There are seven beliefs a prospect must hold before they will buy: Pain (they have a real gap), Doubt (they cannot fix it alone), Cost (inaction is more expensive than action), Desire (solving this leads to a better future), Money (they can and should invest), Support (the people around them back the decision), Trust (this specific method is the right one). Score this as an integrated structure, not a checklist. Every objection at the close that was not pre-handled is evidence of a failure here.

Scoring bands:
- 1-3: Multiple beliefs unaddressed. Objections at the close caught the caller off guard.
- 4-6: Covered most beliefs but missed at least one that later surfaced as an objection.
- 7: Every relevant belief addressed before the pitch. One was thin and needed handling again at the close.
- 8: Every relevant belief built and tested before the price. The only objections at the close were financial or logistical.
- 9: Beliefs built in an order that made each next one easier, and each checked with a tie-down before the caller moved on.
- 10: The caller pre-handled the exact objection this prospect was going to raise, in the prospect’s own words, before it was raised.

Look for:
- Did the caller introduce doubt ("What's been stopping you from figuring this out by yourself?")?
- Did the caller establish the cost of inaction?
- Did the caller check for partner or decision-maker support early?
- Did the caller uncover past solutions and position against them?
- Did the caller paint the desired future vividly enough to create urgency?
- Name the specific beliefs that were weakest or missing.

### 4. Pitch Precision

Was the pitch custom-built for this prospect, and did it sell the transformation rather than the deliverables?

Two things matter. First, did the pitch reference specific things the prospect said in discovery — their exact words, their numbers, their personal goals — or could it have been delivered to anyone? Second, did the caller pitch the transformation or the deliverables? Describing what you will do is weak. Describing what changes for the prospect as a result is strong. The strong shape is a bridge built from discovery: name the deliverable, connect it to the logical benefit, then to the emotional benefit, and tie down after each pillar — or open with the prospect's own words: "You told me [their pain]... so what we do is [solution]... so that you can [what changes for them]." A pitch assembled this way cannot be generic.

Scoring bands:
- 1-3: Generic pitch. Mostly deliverables. Could have been said to anyone on any call.
- 4-6: Referenced some discovery details but still leaned on deliverables and features.
- 7: Built for this prospect — their numbers and their words — with tie-downs, but slid into a list of deliverables for part of it.
- 8: Every pillar tied to something the prospect said, transformation before deliverables, tied down after each.
- 9: Also kept short: pitched only the parts that answered this prospect’s situation and left the rest out.
- 10: The pitch quoted the prospect’s own words back to them as each pillar landed, and the bridge from their pain to the outcome was explicit every time.

Look for:
- Did the caller use the prospect's own words in the pitch?
- Did the caller tie the solution to the prospect's specific desired outcome rather than a generic one?
- Did the caller tie down after each pillar ("Make sense?" / "What questions do you have on that?")?
- Did the caller pitch deliverables instead of transformation?
- Was the delivery section kept simple, or did it overwhelm the prospect?

### 5. Tension Management

Could the caller hold tension without becoming needy?

This is the difference between people who have great conversations and people who close. Tension is the silence after the price, the direct question about money, the push-back on "I need to think about it". Neediness shows as a rushed tone, excitement when the prospect agrees, accepting a counter-offer at once, overpromising, filling the silence after the price, or softening the number ("it's only", "but there's a payment plan") before the prospect has reacted. Read silence off the timestamps: the gap between the price line and the caller's next line. Three seconds or more before the caller speaks again is holding; speaking again inside that with nothing from the prospect in between is filling. Where the price was stated once and the prospect answered at once, there was no silence to hold — score the other tense moments on the call, and if there were none, return 0.

Scoring bands:
- 1-3: Broke tension repeatedly — filled silences, softened the price before the prospect reacted, backed off at the first push.
- 4-6: Held some tense moments but folded on at least one that mattered: the price, a hard money question, or "I need to think about it".
- 7: Held the price and the hard questions. One soft moment that did not cost the deal.
- 8: Held every tense moment. Said the number, stopped, waited. Asked about money directly and let the answer come.
- 9: Also held when the prospect pushed a second time, without hardening or apologising.
- 10: Used a silence or a direct question at the moment of decision to move the prospect — chosen, not endured.

Look for:
- After the price line, when is the caller’s next line, and what did the prospect say in between?
- Did the caller ever sound like they needed the sale?
- Did the caller ask about money directly ("what do you have available right now?")?
- Did the caller back down from a push too early?

### 6. Objection Resolution

Did the caller find the real objection before answering it?

An objection has a surface and a cause. The job is to pace the first objection without reacting, find out whether the prospect is sold on the solution before dealing with money or a partner ("finances aside, is this what you need?"), handle any doubt about the solution first, and only then handle money or partner. The biggest error is treating a certainty problem as a money problem: a prospect who is not sold gets a payment plan instead of an answer. A scheduled second step — a deposit now and the balance on a named date, or a partner conversation booked with the prospect’s commitment to decide on a named day — is a resolution when the caller secured the commitment and the date. It is a deferral when the caller offered it to end the conversation. Where the prospect raised no objection there is nothing to score: return 0.

Scoring bands:
- 1-3: Reacted to the surface. Never isolated the real objection. Went to payment plans before checking the prospect was sold.
- 4-6: Paced and isolated, but handled in the wrong order, or answered a surface objection while the real one went unresolved.
- 7: Paced, isolated and handled in the right order. One objection was answered rather than uncovered.
- 8: Clean sequence on every objection: paced, certainty tied down, doubt before money, discovery referenced.
- 9: Also anticipated: the caller named the objection before the prospect did, or turned the prospect’s own earlier words into the answer.
- 10: An objection became the reason to buy, and the caller did it without a rebuttal.

Look for:
- Did the caller pace the first objection rather than rebutting it?
- Did the caller isolate the real objection?
- Did the caller use consistency ("You told me earlier that...")?
- Did the caller handle doubt about the solution before money?
- If a second step was scheduled, did the caller get a commitment and a date, or just an exit?
- Was the tonality neutral and grounded, or harsh, quick and defensive?

### 7. Qualification

Did the caller establish early whether this is a real prospect, and act on what they found?

Key areas: can this person decide alone or is there a partner, do they have financial capacity, is the timing right, are they shopping around. This is also about acting on what you find. Identifying that a partner exists and then ignoring it until it surfaces as an objection at the close is a failure. Disqualifying too early and missing a real opportunity is the opposite failure. A two-step close is not a failure of qualification when the caller set it up: a deposit taken and the balance dated, or a partner conversation booked with the prospect committed to a decision on a named day. It is a failure when a partner or a money gap was found and the caller pitched as if it were not there, or when a follow-up was booked because the caller had no next move. A follow-up with no commitment attached rarely closes.

Scoring bands:
- 1-3: No qualification. Pitched everyone the same regardless of signals.
- 4-6: Some qualification but did not act on it — a partner or a money gap surfaced and the pitch went ahead as if it had not.
- 7: Decision-maker, money and timing checked. One signal found late or acted on late.
- 8: Qualified early and decisively, and the call was shaped by it: one call or two steps chosen on the evidence, pitch depth matched to it.
- 9: Also disqualified or paused correctly where the evidence said to, without losing the prospect.
- 10: Qualification set up the close so completely that nothing at the end was a surprise, and the caller could name the exact reason this prospect would buy.

Look for:
- Did the caller check for decision-maker status (partner, spouse, board)?
- Did the caller financially pre-qualify before going deep?
- Did the caller recognise an uncloseable prospect and handle it efficiently?
- If a second step was scheduled, did it carry a deposit or a dated commitment?
- Was a follow-up booked because the prospect needed one, or because the caller had run out of moves?

### 8. Strategic Awareness

Did the caller read the room and adapt in real time?

The meta-dimension. Were there moments the caller clearly missed? Did they misread the prospect's certainty level? Did they stay too long in discovery when the prospect was ready to move, or rush the pitch when more discovery was needed? This is about pattern recognition and real-time adaptation.

Scoring bands:
- 1-3: Major misreads. Moved to pitch when the prospect was not ready, or spent forty minutes in discovery on a prospect who was already sold. Missed obvious buying signals or red flags.
- 4-6: Read the room mostly right but missed secondary signals. Some mismatch between the prospect’s energy and the caller’s approach.
- 7: Read the prospect accurately and adapted. One signal missed, or one phase run long.
- 8: Accurate reads throughout. Pace matched the prospect; pushed and backed off at the right moments.
- 9: Also changed the plan mid-call on the evidence — shortened discovery, skipped a pillar, moved to the close early — and it was the right change.
- 10: Spotted the one moment that decided the call as it happened and acted on it. The moment you would name in this review and the caller’s move are the same line.

Look for:
- Were there buying signals that went unnoticed?
- Were there red flags that got ignored?
- Did the caller spend too long or too little on any phase?
- Was there a moment where a different move would have changed the outcome?


## Lead quality — eight factors, scored out of 100

The eight dimensions above score the caller. This section scores the lead they were handed. It exists so a bad number can be traced to the right cause: a closer who executed badly, or a prospect who was never going to buy. Score only what the prospect said and did, never what the caller asserted on their behalf. Where a subject came up and the answer was vague, score the band the evidence supports. Where it never came up at all — the caller did not ask and the prospect did not volunteer — return 0 and say what was missing. 0 means not assessed, never a score of zero; it is left blank on the tracker and drops out of the Lead Score. A guessed middle score here is worse than no score, because it makes an unknown lead look like an average one.

Each factor is scored from 1 to its own maximum — a number only, with no written reason of its own. The factors carry different maximums because they do not matter equally: they sum to 100 when every one is scored. Your reading of the lead as a whole goes in lead_read, and that is where the evidence for these numbers belongs.

### pain_severity — out of 15 · maps to the Pain belief

How badly is the prospect's current situation actually hurting them?

Score the pain the prospect described, not the pain the caller asserted on their behalf. Three things raise it: how long it has gone on, whether it is quantified in real numbers, and whether it reaches past the business into their life, health, relationships or self-image. A prospect who is mildly dissatisfied buys nothing regardless of who is on the call.

Scoring bands:
- 1-5: Mild dissatisfaction, or pain the caller described for them. No duration, no numbers, no personal cost.
- 6-10: Real and sustained problem, stated in their own words, but contained to the business and not quantified.
- 11-15: Quantified, long-running and personal. They named what it costs them and it reached beyond work.

### urgency — out of 15 · maps to the Cost belief

Is there a reason this has to be solved now rather than in six months?

Separate internal urgency — frustration, a self-imposed deadline, being fed up — from an external forcing function, such as a contract ending, a funded account expiring, a season closing, or money running out on a date they named. External beats internal every time. General frustration with no deadline is the most common false positive in this whole rubric: it feels urgent on the call and defers the moment the call ends.

Scoring bands:
- 1-5: No urgency. Exploring, curious, or 'sometime this year'. Nothing changes if they do nothing.
- 6-10: Internal urgency only — real frustration and a wish to move faster, but no date and nothing forcing it.
- 11-15: An external forcing function with a date attached, or a cost of waiting the prospect themselves quantified.

### desire_clarity — out of 15 · maps to the Desire belief

Can the prospect describe the specific outcome they want?

"I want to be profitable" is vague. "Five thousand a month by March so I can hand in my notice" is clear. Score the specificity of the destination — a number, a date, a named change in their life — and whether an emotional anchor is attached to it. A prospect who cannot name the outcome cannot recognise it when it is sold to them.

Scoring bands:
- 1-5: No stated outcome, or a direction so general it could belong to anyone.
- 6-10: Clear direction and a real motivation, but no number, no date, or no concrete picture of the destination.
- 11-15: A specific outcome with a figure or a deadline, tied to something that matters to them personally.

### solution_belief — out of 15 · maps to the Trust belief

Does the prospect believe this kind of solution works?

Belief in the category and the method — mentorship, coaching, the approach being sold — not yet belief in this particular provider. A prospect who arrived already sold on the concept and is only choosing between providers scores high. Active scepticism about the whole category, or a belief that everyone selling it is a scammer, scores low.

Scoring bands:
- 1-5: Sceptical of the category. Treats the approach, or the people selling it, as unproven or dishonest.
- 6-10: Open to it without conviction. Willing to hear the case but has not decided the approach is the answer.
- 11-15: Pre-sold on the method. Sought it out, named it themselves, or referenced others it worked for.

### self_efficacy — out of 10 · maps to the Doubt belief

Does the prospect believe they can execute it, while accepting they need help to do so?

This factor is not monotonic, and scoring it as 'more is better' gets it backwards. Some doubt is required to buy: a prospect certain they can fix it alone has no reason to pay anyone. But a prospect who does not believe they could succeed even with help will not commit either — they defer, because committing risks proving something about themselves. The buyable zone is the middle: they accept they need outside help and still believe they can do the work. Score how favourable the position is for a close, not how much confidence is present.

Scoring bands:
- 1-3: Either extreme. Certain they can do it alone and just gathering information, or visibly doubting they can succeed at all.
- 4-6: Leaning to one end. Mostly self-reliant, or carrying self-doubt that is likely to surface as hesitation at the close.
- 7-10: The buyable zone. Openly needs help, still believes they can execute once they have it.

### authority — out of 10 · maps to the Support belief

Can this person decide alone, and do the people around them back it?

Two parts, both required. Can they say yes today without anyone else's approval, and will the people close to them — partner, spouse, business partner — support the decision rather than talk them out of it afterwards. Speaking in the first person throughout with no mention of anyone else is a strong signal; so is explicitly saying a partner is on board.

Scoring bands:
- 1-3: Someone else decides, or a partner was named as an obstacle rather than a supporter.
- 4-6: Probably able to decide but a second party exists and was never resolved either way.
- 7-10: Sole decision-maker, or the people around them were named and confirmed as supportive.

### financial_capacity — out of 10 · maps to the Money belief

Can the prospect actually pay what this offer costs?

Capacity, not willingness — willingness belongs to the beliefs above. Look for what they said about income, savings, existing spend on similar things, or what they have already invested in trying to solve this. Having previously paid for a comparable programme is the strongest single signal. Treat "I'd have to find it" as low. If money never came up on the call at all, return 0 rather than a middle score.

Scoring bands:
- 1-3: Stated or clearly implied inability to pay at this level, or the price would be a genuine hardship.
- 4-6: Mixed or indirect signals. They implied they could find it, or spend on something adjacent, without confirming capacity at this level.
- 7-10: Demonstrated capacity — current income, prior spend on something comparable, or funds explicitly available.

### icp_fit — out of 10

Is this the kind of buyer the offer is actually built for?

Judge against the offer context at the top of this prompt: the situation, stage, niche and starting point the offer is designed for. This is the one factor that is about fit rather than state of mind, and it is the one marketing controls most directly. A perfect-fit prospect with no urgency is a nurture problem; a poor-fit prospect with high urgency is a targeting problem.

Scoring bands:
- 1-3: Wrong buyer. Outside the niche, at the wrong stage, or wanting something the offer does not do.
- 4-6: Adjacent. Recognisably in the market but off the described profile in stage, niche or starting point.
- 7-10: Squarely the intended buyer, matching the offer context on situation, stage and goal.

Patterns worth naming when you see them:
- High Solution Belief with mid Self-Efficacy is the most closeable pattern there is: they believe the method works and know they cannot do it alone. Lead with proof and structure — people like them who got the result.
- High Solution Belief with low Self-Efficacy is coachable but fragile. They will buy the idea and stall on themselves. It needs an identity reframe before the price, not a payment plan after it.
- Low Solution Belief with high Self-Efficacy is the hardest pattern. They think they can do it alone and doubt the method. Nothing closes until doubt is built first.
- High everything with low Urgency is a real buyer on the wrong day. The mistake is discounting; the fix is a forcing function or a scheduled return.
- High Pain with low Financial Capacity is the most expensive lead to chase. It feels closeable on the call and does not pay.


## Objections raised

Record which objections were actually voiced, so the pattern across calls is visible. An objection counts when the prospect raised it, whether or not the caller resolved it. If the call ended before any objection surfaced, answer with None raised and leave the primary objection null.

- **Price** (Money) — The number itself is the problem — too expensive, cannot afford it, needs it cheaper.
- **Timing** (Cost) — Wants to start later. A better month, after something else finishes, not right now.
- **Partner** (Support) — Needs to speak to a spouse, business partner, or anyone else before deciding.
- **Think about it** (Doubt) — Wants to defer with no reason given. The classic stand-in for an objection they did not name.
- **Doubts the method** (Trust) — Not convinced this approach works, or not convinced it works when this provider delivers it.
- **Doubts themselves** (Doubt) — Believes the offer works but not that they personally will follow through on it.
- **Tried before** (Trust) — A previous course, coach or programme failed them and this looks like the same thing.
- **Comparing options** (Trust) — Weighing a named alternative, or shopping the category before committing.
- **No time** (Cost) — Believes it works and cannot see where the hours to do it would come from.
- **None raised** — No objection was voiced — including calls that ended before the offer was made.

List every objection the prospect actually voiced. Then name the single one that decided the call — the one that, left unresolved, is why it did not close. When the call closed, or no objection was raised, the primary objection is null.

## Bonus flags

- **value_leak** — Did the caller give away consulting or strategy before getting a commitment?
- **follow_up_trap** — Was a follow-up booked with no deposit and no dated commitment attached, when the prospect was in a position to decide on the call?
- **price_drop_too_early** — Was the price stated before the caller had checked the prospect was sold on the solution? A deposit asked for after a certainty tie-down is not an early drop; a price named before the pitch, or before any tie-down, is.
- **weakest_belief** — Which of the seven beliefs did the caller build least on this call? The belief the caller left weakest, not the one the prospect arrived weakest on. Answer with one of: Pain, Doubt, Cost, Desire, Money, Support, Trust, None.

## Written feedback

- **best_moment** — The single strongest move the caller made. Quote it.
- **biggest_miss** — The single highest-leverage thing that could have been different. Quote it.
- **the_moment** — Identify the single highest-leverage point on the call where a different move would have changed the outcome. Quote the transcript with its [mm:ss], explain what happened, what should have happened, and why it matters. Two to four sentences.
- **next_call_drill** — One specific, concrete behaviour the caller can apply on their very next call. Not "work on your discovery" but "when the prospect gives a surface-level answer, say 'tell me more about that' and then wait." One or two sentences.
- **lead_read** — What the lead-quality factors mean for how this prospect should be handled — written about the prospect, not the caller. Name the pattern the factors form, say what it implies for the next conversation with them, and give the one move that fits it. Two to four sentences. Where one of the listed patterns applies, use it; where none does, describe what you actually see.

## Commercial fields

Also extract the commercial facts of the call. Outcome definitions: Customer = said yes. BAMFAM = follow-up scheduled. No offer made = unqualified or no offer given. No deal = offered but declined. No show = did not attend. REFUND = cancelled after buying.

For any commercial field the transcript does not establish, return null rather than guessing. `lead_source` cannot be null: when the transcript never establishes where the prospect came from, answer Unknown rather than guessing.


`collected_on_call` is money that actually changed hands **during the call** — a card taken while the prospect was still on the line. A deal that was agreed on the call but paid afterwards collected nothing on the call, so it is 0, not the price. Payments that land later are recorded by hand afterwards and are not your job to predict.

`currency` is the currency the price was quoted in, one of USD, EUR, GBP. Take it from how the caller said the number — "three thousand euros" or "€3,000" is EUR, "$6,000" is USD. When no currency is stated anywhere in the call, answer USD. Every money field you return must be in that one currency; never convert.

`summary` is two to three sentences on what happened, written for someone who did not attend the call.
